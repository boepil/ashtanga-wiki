# SI Joint Instability in Asymmetric Poses

![[ChatGPT Image May 6, 2026, 08_39_11 PM.png]]
### 1. Pelvic Stability and Coronal Alignment

**Lack**  
Lack of pelvic stability (Gluteus Medius/Minimus strength) and insufficient internal rotation capacity in the back leg humerus/femur to maintain a level sacrum.

**Compensation**  
Shearing force at the sacrum; the back hip "dropping" or rotating excessively forward/back to "find" range; and over-rotation of the lumbar spine to compensate for a "stuck" pelvis in twists.

**Where It Shows Up**  
- **Movement:** Walking on uneven surfaces, stepping sideways.  
- **Yoga:** [[Utthita Trikonasana]], [[Parivrtta Trikonasana]], [[Utthita Parsvakonasana]], and asymmetric seated twists like [[Marichyasana]].

**What to Train**  
- **Gluteus Medius/Minimus:** To maintain a level pelvis and prevent the "hip drop."
- **Internal Rotation (Back Leg):** In standing poses, the back leg must maintain a degree of internal rotation and grounding through the outer heel to stabilize the sacrum.
- **Mula Bandha:** To provide the "root" stability that keeps the pelvic halves moving as a single unit.

**Yoga Expression**  
A dull, nagging ache in the back of the pelvis (just to the side of the sacrum); uneven hips in forward folds; and a feeling of "disconnect" or "wobble" between the legs and the spine.

**Meta Insight**  
"Mobility (sacroiliac) substituting for stability (pelvic/hip)." The system allows for minute, potentially injurious movement at the SI joint because the hips are too tight or the stabilizing muscles too weak to hold the pelvis steady.

---

## Diagnostic Cue
In Trikonasana, can you keep your sacrum flat and your hips level? If your back hip "hikes up" toward your ribs or if you feel a "tweak" in the back of your pelvis, you are likely shearing your SI joint rather than rotating at the hip.

## Common Misinterpretation
Practitioners often try to "square the hips" in poses where the hips should naturally be at an angle (like Trikonasana). Forcing the hips to be perfectly "square" or "open" against the anatomical limit of the joint often creates the very shearing force that destabilizes the SI joint. The goal is "functional stability," not aesthetic symmetry.
