# Skeletal System

## Summary
The skeletal system is the foundational framework of the body, consisting of approximately 206 bones that act as levers for movement. In yoga, the health of the skeleton is maintained through **joint congruency** and an understanding of **skeletal variation** [1, 10]. The central pillar is the spine, whose natural curves and potential for **axial extension** define the structural integrity of the practice [1, 6].

## Detail

### The Spine: The Central Pillar
The adult spine has four natural S-shaped curves—cervical and lumbar (lordotic) and thoracic and sacral (kyphotic)—which act as a spring to absorb shock and maintain the body's center of gravity [1].

- **Axial Extension: The "Fifth Movement"**: 
    - Defined as the simultaneous reduction of both primary and secondary curves to increase the overall length of the vertebral column [5, 6].
    - This "unnatural" movement requires conscious effort and the coordination of the [[Bandha_Overview|Bandhas]] to create a stable core state known as **Mahamudra** [7, 8].
- **Intervertebral Disks**: Movement must be mindful to avoid uneven compression. Practice involves moving the spine through flexion, extension, rotation, and lateral flexion, ideally preceded by axial extension to create space [5, 6].

### Skeletal Variation and Individual Limits
The appearance of a pose is dictated by inherent structural factors that vary significantly between individuals [7]:
- **Femoral Neck-Shaft Angle**: Can vary from 110° to 150°, altering the range of motion in poses like [[Utthita Trikonasana]] [35].
- **Carrying Angle of the Elbow**: Natural variation (0 to 23 degrees) affects hand placement and the appearance of "straight" arms in weight-bearing [8].
- **Tibial Torsion**: Determines the natural "point" of the feet in standing poses [35].

### Functional Segments
- **Pelvic Bowl**: The mechanical center of the body. Capable of anterior tilt (increasing lumbar curve) and posterior tilt (stabilizing the lower back) [15].
- **Shoulder Girdle**: A shallow "golf ball on a tee" structure (glenoid fossa) that relies on dynamic muscular stabilization rather than deep bone-on-bone contact [14].
