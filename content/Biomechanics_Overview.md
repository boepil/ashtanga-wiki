# Biomechanics Overview

## Summary
Biomechanics is the study of the body's mechanical function—how bones, joints, muscles, and connective tissue interact to produce and control movement. In Ashtanga Yoga, biomechanical principles help distinguish between safe functional alignment and aesthetic ideals that may not suit every body. Key concepts include closed-chain mechanics, the tension-compression spectrum, scapulohumeral rhythm, and the time-dependent behavior of connective tissue.

## Detail

### Closed-Chain Biomechanics
![[chaturanga_biomechanics.png]]

A closed kinetic chain exercise is one where the distal segment (hand or foot) is fixed against a surface, and forces are transmitted proximally through the limb and trunk. Chaturanga Dandasana functions as an upper-limb closed-chain exercise:
- The hands are fixed against the mat; movement occurs at the shoulder, elbow, and trunk relative to that fixed point.
- The rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) dynamically stabilizes the humeral head, preventing excessive anterior translation during the lowering phase.
- The serratus anterior maintains scapular contact with the ribcage and prevents winging.
- Lumbopelvic stability is essential: the core must resist lumbar extension to protect the lower back.

### Scapulohumeral Rhythm and the Rotator Cuff
The shoulder joint is the most mobile but least stable joint in the body—a "golf ball on a tee" structure that relies on muscular control rather than bony congruence.
- **Scapulohumeral Rhythm**: For every 2 degrees the arm moves, the scapula rotates 1 degree. Resisting this natural rhythm—by cueing "shoulders down and back" during arm elevation—can disrupt joint congruency and contribute to impingement.
- **Rotator Cuff Function**: The four SITS muscles act as dynamic stabilizers, fine-tuning the humeral head position during movement. In weight-bearing poses (Chaturanga, Handstand, Downward Dog), external rotation of the humerus moves the greater tubercle away from the acromion, preserving subacromial space.
- **Torque for Stability**: Creating rotational "torque" at the hands and arms takes up capsular slack, making the joint tight and stable for load-bearing.

### Pelvic Tilt Biomechanics
The pelvis acts as a biomechanical bridge between the trunk and lower limbs. Its orientation in the sagittal plane directly affects lumbar posture and hip mechanics.
- **Anterior Pelvic Tilt**: The front of the pelvis rotates downward; the iliopsoas, rectus femoris, and erector spinae are the primary drivers. This increases lumbar lordosis and lengthens the abdominals and gluteals.
- **Posterior Pelvic Tilt**: The front of the pelvis rotates upward; the rectus abdominis, obliques, gluteus maximus, and hamstrings generate this action. It reduces lumbar lordosis and places the hip extensors in a mechanically advantageous position.
- **Ashtanga Application**: In backbends, controlled anterior tilt is necessary for hip extension; in forward folds, a posterior tilt initiated by the hamstrings deepens the fold while protecting the lumbar spine.

### The Windlass Effect (Foot Biomechanics)
![[foot_windlass_mechanism.png]]

The windlass effect describes the stabilization of the foot during push-off:
- As the toes dorsiflex during the late stance phase of gait (or the push-off in vinyasa), the plantar fascia tightens around the metatarsal heads, elevating the medial longitudinal arch.
- This transforms the foot from a flexible shock absorber into a rigid lever for efficient propulsion.
- **Pada Bandha correlates**: The lift of the "heart of the foot" initiates this mechanism, creating a stable foundation for weight-bearing poses and enhancing force transfer through the kinetic chain.

### Tissue Mechanics: Creep and Stress Relaxation
Connective tissue (fascia, tendons, ligaments) is viscoelastic—its response to load is time-dependent.
- **Creep**: Slow, progressive lengthening of tissue under a constant load. In practice, this occurs during long-held passive stretches.
- **Stress Relaxation**: The decrease in resistance within a tissue held at a fixed length. The initial "pull" in a forward fold gradually diminishes as the tissue acclimates.
- **Application to Practice**: Quick movements (Surya Namaskara) are too fast to affect viscoelastic properties and can trigger the stretch reflex. Longer holds (3–5 minutes or more) are necessary to reach deeper non-contractile elements and promote lasting elongation.

### Principles of Flexibility
Gaining functional range of motion involves both neurological and structural adaptation:
- **Reciprocal Inhibition**: Actively contracting the antagonist muscle triggers a reflex relaxation in the target muscle. In Paschimottanasana, engaging the quadriceps signals the hamstrings to relax.
- **Sarcomerogenesis**: Consistent practice can trigger the addition of sarcomeres (contractile units) in series, increasing functional muscle length.
- **Neurological Adaptation**: Most increases in range of motion come from the brain becoming less sensitized to the discomfort of a stretch, rather than immediate structural lengthening.
