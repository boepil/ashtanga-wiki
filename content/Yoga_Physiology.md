# Yoga Physiology

## Summary
Yoga physiology bridges the gap between ancient yogic concepts (chakras, nadis, bandhas, koshas) and modern anatomical science. Where tradition maps the subtle energy body, modern physiology finds correlates in nerve plexuses, fascial meridians, diaphragmatic coordination, and autonomic regulation. Understanding these connections allows practitioners to use the physical practice as a precise tool for systemic regulation.

## Detail

### The Three Diaphragms: The Functional Cylinder
The body operates as a pressurized "functional cylinder" supported by three horizontal diaphragms. Their coordination regulates intra-abdominal pressure (IAP), which provides structural support for the spine and lightness for vinyasa.

| Diaphragm | Location | Bandha Correlate | Function |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Vocal** (Glottis) | Throat | Jalandhara Bandha | Regulates airflow and top-down pressure via Ujjayi |
| **Respiratory** | Thorax/Abdomen | Uddiyana Bandha | Primary driver of breath; descent increases IAP |
| **Pelvic** (Pelvic Floor) | Pelvis | Mula Bandha | Base of the cylinder; lifting stabilizes the foundation |

- **Piston Rhythm**: These three structures move in a synchronized rhythm. Co-activating them creates the "spinal airbag" that stabilizes the lumbar spine from the inside out.
- **Ujjayi as Valve Control**: The glottic constriction of Ujjayi breathing provides back-pressure that regulates the rate of airflow and maintains internal pressure throughout movement.

### Nerve Plexus Mapping to Chakras
The seven chakras of yogic anatomy correlate closely with major nerve plexuses—concentrations of neural activity where electrical signaling is highest.

| Chakra | Nerve Plexus | Location | Physiological Association |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Muladhara** (Root) | Coccygeal Plexus | Base of spine | Pelvic floor, elimination, survival instincts |
| **Svadhisthana** (Sacral) | Sacral Plexus | Lower abdomen | Reproductive organs, fluid regulation |
| **Manipura** (Solar Plexus) | Celiac Plexus | Upper abdomen | Digestion, adrenal stress response, "belly brain" |
| **Anahata** (Heart) | Cardiac Plexus | Chest | Heart rate, respiration, emotional regulation |
| **Vishuddha** (Throat) | Cervical Plexus | Neck | Thyroid, vocal cords, metabolic regulation |
| **Ajna** (Third Eye) | Carotid/Cavernous Plexus | Forehead | Pituitary, autonomic balance |
| **Sahasrara** (Crown) | Cortical Centers | Cranium | Higher cognitive integration |

- **Anatomical Basis**: When a practitioner focuses on a chakra, they are stimulating the corresponding nerve hub and its associated endocrine and autonomic functions.
- **Nadi Shodhana**: Alternate nostril breathing balances the autonomic nervous system by directly influencing the vagus nerve and the sympathetic chain.

### The BPSS Model (Biopsychosocial-Spiritual)
The BPSS model mirrors the yogic Kosha system: it views health as a dynamic interplay between multiple layers of experience rather than a purely mechanical problem.

- **Biological**: Physical alignment, joint health, muscular strength—the asana practice.
- **Psychological**: Mental focus (Dharana), emotional resilience, quieting the mind through breath.
- **Social**: Connection to lineage (Parampara), the teacher-student relationship, the collective energy of the Mysore room.
- **Spiritual**: Daily practice (Sadhana), sense of purpose, connection to something larger.

**Application to Injury**: A rotator cuff injury is rarely "just" a torn tendon. A BPSS perspective asks: Is the root cause biological (weakness), psychological (anxiety driving tension), social (feeling isolated in practice), or spiritual (loss of meaning)? Recovery must address all layers.

### Pranayama Physiology
Pranayama serves as a voluntary override for the autonomic nervous system.

| Breath Pattern | Primary Effect | Physiological Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Extended Exhalation (Ujjayi) | Parasympathetic activation | Vagus nerve stimulation, HRV increase |
| Extended Inhalation (Kumbhaka) | Sympathetic activation | Increased alertness, blood CO2 regulation |
| Nadi Shodhana | Bilateral autonomic balance | Hemispheric synchronization |

- **Vagal Lever**: Slow, deep breathing with extended exhalations stimulates the vagus nerve, shifting the body toward parasympathetic dominance.
- **CO2 Regulation**: Proper pranayama maintains an ideal balance of carbon dioxide, which regulates blood pH, electrolyte balance, and oxygen delivery (Bohr effect).
- **Nasal Resistance**: Nasal breathing provides 50% more resistance than mouth breathing, improving lung volume and oxygen uptake.

### The Reward Mechanism and Neurochemistry
Consistent practice affects the brain's reward system and supports neuroplasticity.

| Chemical | Role in Practice | Effect |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Dopamine** | Motivation, anticipation, reinforcement | Rewards consistent practice; builds discipline |
| **Serotonin** | Mood regulation, satiety | Balances cravings; supports emotional stability |
| **Cortisol** | Primary stress hormone | Lowered through consistent practice (HPA axis regulation) |
| **Endorphins** | Natural pain modulation | Released during sustained effort (practitioner's "high") |

- **Neuroplasticity**: Regular practice leads to functional and structural changes in the prefrontal cortex (decision-making) and amygdala (emotional regulation).
- **Tapas as Positive Stress**: The daily discipline of practice acts as hormesis—a "positive stress" that strengthens the system's resilience and rewires long-term mental habits.
