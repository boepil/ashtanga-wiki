Breath holding under effort (involuntary Kumbhaka) is a sign of high CO2 sensitivity and poor metabolic efficiency (McKeown, The Oxygen Advantage, p. 55). In Ashtanga vinyasa, this manifests as gasping after intense transitions or "locking" the breath to create stability. This protocol retrains the chemoreceptors to tolerate higher levels of CO2, allowing for calm, nasal breathing even under significant physical load (Nestor, Breath, p. 210).

## Overview

## Guidelines
- Frequency: Daily (especially nasal breathing awareness).
- Session time: 15-20 minutes of dedicated training.
- Principle: CO2 Tolerance. The urge to breathe is triggered by CO2 buildup, not lack of oxygen. By increasing CO2 tolerance, we reduce the "breathlessness" response in vinyasa (McKeown, p. 128).

## Pain/Safety guide
- **Dizziness/Lightheadedness:** Normal during air hunger drills, but stop if it becomes overwhelming.
- **Tinnitus (Ringing in ears):** Reduce intensity immediately.
- **Panic/Anxiety:** Use "Box Breathing" to down-regulate the nervous system (Nestor, p. 205).

## Phase A — Prep / Mobility (5-8 min)

### BOLT Score Assessment
(McKeown, The Oxygen Advantage, p. 55-60)
- How: Take a normal breath in and out. Hold the nose. Time how many seconds until you feel the *first definite desire* to breathe or the first involuntary contraction of the breathing muscles.
- Target: 40 seconds is the functional goal. If under 20 seconds, focus strictly on Phase B.

### Diaphragmatic Release (Manual)
(Kaminoff, Yoga Anatomy, p. 124)
- How: Sit comfortably. Gently hook the fingers under the ribcage. Inhale softly into the fingers, then exhale and allow the fingers to sink slightly deeper.
- Duration: 2 minutes.
- Target: Restores sliding surfaces of the diaphragm and reduces tension in the "Deep Front Line" (Myers, p. 338).

### Nasal Breathing Only (Warm-up)
(Nestor, Breath, p. 210)
- How: For the first 10 minutes of any physical activity (walking, Sun Salutations), breathe strictly through the nose. If you must open your mouth, slow down.
- Target: Switches the body from sympathetic (emergency) to parasympathetic (efficiency) mode.

## Phase B — Strength / Tolerance (20-25 min)

### Breathe Light to Breathe Right
(McKeown, The Oxygen Advantage, p. 142)
- How: Sit tall. Place hands on chest and belly. Reduce the volume of each breath until you feel a "tolerable hunger for air." Maintain this for 5-10 minutes.
- Target: Resets the respiratory center's sensitivity to CO2.

### Exhale Holds (Air Hunger Training)
(McKeown, The Oxygen Advantage, p. 128)
- How: After a normal exhalation, hold the breath while walking. Count the number of paces. Stop when the hunger for air is strong but not panicky. Recover with 2-3 calm breaths.
- Reps: 5-8 repetitions.
- Target: Simulates the metabolic stress of a vinyasa transition without the mechanical complexity.

### Vagal Tone Restoration (Long Exhale)
(Nestor, Breath, p. 200)
- How: Inhale for 4 seconds, exhale for 6-8 seconds. Focus on the slight pause at the bottom of the exhale.
- Duration: 5 minutes.
- Target: Directly stimulates the Vagus nerve to lower heart rate and blood pressure.

## Phase C — Control / Integration (5-10 min)

### Ujjayi with Diaphragmatic Awareness
(Kaminoff, Yoga Anatomy, p. 188)
- How: Perform Ujjayi breath while focusing on the expansion of the *lower* ribs (horizontal breathing) rather than the lifting of the chest (vertical breathing).
- Target: Ensures the diaphragm is providing structural support (IAP) without restricting the heart.

### Box Breathing (CO2 Desensitization)
(Nestor, Breath, p. 205)
- How: Inhale 4s, Hold 4s, Exhale 4s, Hold 4s.
- Duration: 5 minutes.
- Target: Used by Navy SEALs to maintain focus under extreme stress; applies directly to difficult asanas.

## Progression
(McKeown, The Oxygen Advantage, p. 55)
Criteria for progress:
- **BOLT Score Increase:** You should see a 5-second increase in your BOLT score every 2-3 weeks of consistent practice.
- **Nasal Dominance:** Advance when you can complete the entire Primary Series (up to Navasana) using only nasal breathing.

## When to seek help
- Persistent shortness of breath at rest.
- History of asthma or COPD (consult a doctor before doing air hunger drills).
- fainting or near-fainting during practice.
