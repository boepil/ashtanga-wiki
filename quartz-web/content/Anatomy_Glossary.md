# Anatomy and Biomechanics Glossary for Ashtanga Yoga

This glossary is a source-grounded reference for respiratory physiology, nervous-system regulation, and movement as they relate to Ashtanga Yoga. It complements [[Anatomy_Overview]], [[Biomechanics_Overview]], and [[Respiratory_Anatomy]].

The terms below are scientific or anatomical terms. Sanskrit names are included only when established by the source material; for this glossary, no Sanskrit equivalent is established for the scientific terms listed.

## Movement and Breathing

The practice’s respiratory efficiency depends on quiet nasal breathing, appropriate carbon-dioxide regulation, and coordination between respiration and cardiovascular rhythm. These mechanisms should be read alongside [[Breath_Awareness_And_Anatomy]], [[Ujjayi_Pranayama]], and [[Tristana]].

| Term | Definition | Relevance to Ashtanga | Sanskrit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Inhalation/exhalation ratio** | The temporal relationship between the inspiratory and expiratory phases. | A relatively longer exhalation can support parasympathetic regulation during strenuous transitions. | Not established in the sources |
| **Nasal breathing** | Breathing through the nares, which filters, warms, and humidifies air while adding resistance to airflow. | Supports quieter breathing and may reduce the tendency toward chronic overbreathing during practice. | Not established in the sources |
| **Bohr effect** | The relationship in which increased carbon dioxide and acidity reduce hemoglobin’s affinity for oxygen. | Carbon dioxide availability helps working tissues receive oxygen; breathing should not be treated as simply maximizing oxygen intake. | Not established in the sources |
| **Cardiorespiratory synchronization** | Temporal coupling between the respiratory cycle and heart-rate rhythm. | Slow, rhythmic breathing can coordinate cardiovascular and respiratory work during sustained asana. | Not established in the sources |
| **Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA)** | Normal heart-rate variation: acceleration during inhalation and deceleration during exhalation. | A non-invasive indicator associated with vagal regulation and recovery from practice stress. | Not established in the sources |

**Sources:** Gerritsen & Band, *Breath of Life*, “Effects of Respiration in Theory and Research” and “The Vagus Nerve and Heart Rate Variability”; McKeown, *The Oxygen Advantage*, Introduction and Chapter 3.

## Trunk and Autonomic Regulation

The diaphragm participates in both respiration and autonomic regulation. These terms connect with [[Bandha_Overview]], [[Uddiyana_Bandha]], [[Jalandhara_Bandha]], and [[Breath_Awareness_And_Anatomy]], but they should not be treated as interchangeable with traditional bandha concepts.

| Term | Definition | Relevance to Ashtanga | Sanskrit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Diaphragm** | The primary muscle of respiration, controlled through both voluntary and autonomic pathways. | Its movement changes thoracic and abdominal pressure and contributes to the breath–movement relationship in vinyasa. | Not established in the sources |
| **Vagus nerve (CN X)** | The tenth cranial nerve; it carries substantial visceral sensory information and contributes to parasympathetic regulation. | Slow, controlled breathing and longer exhalations are discussed as ways of supporting vagal regulation during practice. | Not established in the sources |
| **Thorax** | The chest region enclosed by the ribs, sternum, and thoracic spine. | Excessive upper-chest breathing may accompany sympathetic arousal and inefficient breathing under load. | Not established in the sources |
| **Abdominal respiration** | Breathing in which diaphragm descent produces visible movement of the abdominal wall. | Can be used as a cue for quieter, lower-effort breathing, while recognizing that healthy breathing involves the whole respiratory system. | Not established in the sources |
| **Central autonomic network (CAN)** | A distributed brain network involved in autonomic control, emotion, attention, and internal-state regulation. | Helps explain how breathing, attention, and body awareness can influence the experience of challenging asana. | Not established in the sources |
| **Hering–Breuer reflex** | A respiratory reflex involving pulmonary stretch receptors that helps regulate lung inflation and expiration. | Provides a proposed mechanism by which deep inhalation can influence the following exhalation. | Not established in the sources |

**Sources:** Gerritsen & Band, *Breath of Life*, “Possible Mechanisms,” “Respiratory Stimulation,” “Indirect Route,” and “Changes in CAN Regions Through Contact.”

## Oxygen Transport and Exercise Physiology

These terms provide physiological context for endurance in standing, primary, and intermediate sequences. They complement [[Yoga_Physiology]], [[Breath_Exercises_For_Erector_Spinae]], and [[Asana_Fundamentals]].

| Term | Definition | Relevance to Ashtanga | Sanskrit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Red blood cells and hemoglobin** | Red blood cells carry hemoglobin, the protein that transports oxygen in blood. | Performance depends not only on oxygen saturation but also on how effectively oxygen is released to active tissues. | Not established in the sources |
| **Lactate** | A product of glycolysis that can accumulate during high-intensity work; “lactic acid” is commonly used but is not the precise physiological term for blood and muscle conditions. | Helps describe metabolic demand and the burning sensation associated with strenuous transitions. | Not established in the sources |
| **VO₂ max** | The maximum rate at which the body can take in, transport, and use oxygen during intense exercise. | A general measure of aerobic capacity and endurance, not a direct measure of asana skill. | Not established in the sources |
| **Baroreceptors** | Stretch-sensitive receptors in blood-vessel walls that help regulate blood pressure through the baroreflex. | Relevant to cardiovascular responses during slow breathing, exertion, and inversions; symptoms should not be self-diagnosed from this mechanism. | Not established in the sources |
| **Splanchnic anti-inflammatory pathway** | A proposed autonomic pathway involving visceral and sympathetic regulation of inflammatory responses. | Offers a possible physiological explanation for links between breathing, autonomic state, and recovery, but does not establish a treatment effect. | Not established in the sources |

**Sources:** McKeown, *The Oxygen Advantage*, Introduction and “Bring the Mountain to You”; Gerritsen & Band, *Breath of Life*, “Possible Mechanisms” and “Anti-Inflammation.”

## Airways and Pulmonary Mechanics

| Term | Definition | Relevance to Ashtanga | Sanskrit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pulmonary mechanoreceptors** | Sensory receptors in the lungs and airways that provide the central nervous system with information about airway and lung volume. | Their feedback is one proposed pathway linking breath volume with autonomic regulation. | Not established in the sources |
| **Airways** | The passages that conduct air through the nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi. | Nasal airflow and airway resistance influence breathing effort and respiratory comfort during practice. | Not established in the sources |
| **Lungs** | Paired organs in which ventilation brings air into contact with the gas-exchange surfaces. | Efficient ventilation supports sustained movement, but larger breaths are not automatically better breaths. | Not established in the sources |
| **Tidal volume** | The volume of air moved in and out during a normal breath. | Moderate, quiet tidal volume can help avoid inefficient overbreathing during continuous vinyasa. | Not established in the sources |

**Sources:** Gerritsen & Band, *Breath of Life*, “Possible Mechanisms”; McKeown, *The Oxygen Advantage*, Introduction and “Do More with Less.”

## Brain, Attention, and Interoception

These terms relate to the attentional and self-regulatory dimensions of practice. For related discussions, see [[Pain_Science]], [[Injury_Management]], and [[Joint_Stability]].

| Term | Definition | Relevance to Ashtanga | Sanskrit |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)** | A brain region involved in attention, performance monitoring, cognitive control, and autonomic regulation. | May support sustained attention and monitoring of breath and alignment during transitions. | Not established in the sources |
| **Prefrontal cortex (PFC)** | The brain’s executive-control region, involved in planning, inhibition, and emotional regulation. | Supports deliberate attention and the ability to remain responsive rather than reactive in demanding postures. | Not established in the sources |
| **Insula** | A cortical region strongly involved in interoception—the perception of internal bodily states. | Body-awareness training may improve the practitioner’s ability to notice effort, breath, discomfort, and alignment. | Not established in the sources |
| **Amygdala** | A limbic structure involved in threat appraisal and fear-related stress responses. | Helps explain why unfamiliar or demanding inversions can trigger protective arousal and why calm attention matters. | Not established in the sources |
| **Interoception** | The perception and interpretation of signals arising within the body. | Supports informed self-regulation: noticing breath quality, effort, pain, and recovery rather than overriding them. | Not established in the sources |

**Sources:** Gerritsen & Band, *Breath of Life*, “Body Awareness Training,” “HRV and Cognition,” and “Changes in CAN Regions Through Contact.”
