# Upper Extremity Injuries

## Summary
The upper extremity—shoulder, elbow, wrist, and hand—bears substantial load in Ashtanga Yoga, particularly through weight-bearing in Surya Namaskara, arm balances, and inversions. The shoulder is the most mobile yet least stable joint in the body, relying on dynamic muscular control rather than bony congruence. This makes it vulnerable to impingement, rotator cuff pathology, biceps tendinopathy, and scapular dyskinesis. The wrist and elbow are susceptible to repetitive strain from sustained weight-bearing. Accurate diagnosis requires differentiating between these conditions through clinical assessment and neurologic screening.

## Detail

### Clinical Neurologic Examination of the Upper Extremity
![[upper_extremity_neuro_exam.jpg]]

Before localizing upper extremity pain, a basic neurologic screen establishes whether symptoms are musculoskeletal or neurogenic. Key components include:

| Assessment | Myotome/Dermatome | Reflex |
|:---|:---|:---|
| C5 | Shoulder abduction / Lateral arm | Biceps reflex |
| C6 | Elbow flexion / Lateral forearm & thumb | Brachioradialis reflex |
| C7 | Elbow extension / Middle finger | Triceps reflex |
| C8 | Finger flexion / Medial forearm & little finger | — |
| T1 | Finger abduction / Medial arm | — |

- **Dermatomal mapping**: Pain or paresthesia radiating along specific dermatomes indicates cervical radiculopathy rather than local joint pathology.
- **Myotomal testing**: Weakness in a specific myotome (e.g., C5 shoulder abduction weakness) suggests nerve root involvement.
- **Special orthopedic tests**: Hawkins-Kennedy test (impingement), Neer's sign (impingement), O'Brien's test (labral tear), Speed's test (biceps tendinopathy), and drop arm test (rotator cuff tear).

### Subacromial Impingement
Impingement is the most common shoulder condition in yoga. It occurs when soft tissues (supraspinatus tendon, subacromial bursa, biceps tendon) become compressed between the humeral head and the coracoacromial arch.
- **Mechanism**: In arm elevation, especially with internal rotation (common in Downward Dog and Chaturanga), the subacromial space narrows. Poor scapular control—specifically inadequate upward rotation and posterior tilt—worsens this narrowing.
- **Pain pattern**: Anterolateral shoulder pain, often worse with overhead movements, reaching behind the back, or lying on the affected side. "Painful arc" between 60°–120° of abduction.
- **Yoga modifications**: Externally rotate the humerus in weight-bearing poses (Chaturanga, Downward Dog). Keep the arms slightly wider than shoulder width. Avoid deep shoulder flexion until scapular control is restored.
- **Conservative management**: Rest from aggravating movements, physical therapy focusing on scapular stabilizers (serratus anterior, lower trapezius, rhomboids), rotator cuff strengthening, and addressing posterior capsule tightness.

### Rotator Cuff Tears
Rotator cuff tears range from partial-thickness fraying to full-thickness detachment of one or more tendons from the humeral footprint.
- **Distinction from impingement**: Impingement often precedes and contributes to rotator cuff tears. A tear should be suspected when there is persistent weakness, night pain, or a positive drop arm test. MRI or ultrasound confirms the diagnosis.
- **Partial-thickness tears**: Often managed conservatively with relative rest, eccentric loading, and scapular retraining.
- **Full-thickness tears**: May require surgical repair if conservative management fails. Post-surgical yoga return requires graduated loading: passive range of motion first, then active assisted, then resisted strengthening, then return to weight-bearing over 6–12 months.
- **Yoga modifications for known tears**: Skip Chaturanga/Upward Dog transitions entirely. Perform Downward Dog with hands on blocks or forearms (Dolphin Pose). Avoid deep shoulder flexion in backbends.

### Biceps Tendinopathy
The long head of the biceps tendon is particularly vulnerable in Ashtanga due to the repetitive nature of Chaturanga.
- **Mechanism**: The tendon originates at the glenoid, curves over the humeral head, and sits in the bicipital groove. In Chaturanga, when the shoulder moves into extension past the trunk, the tendon compresses against the humerus. This is worse with poor core stability and scapular winging.
- **Symptoms**: Anterior shoulder pain that may radiate down the biceps, worse with repetitive Chaturanga. Loss of shoulder strength. Often co-occurs with rotator cuff tears (80% of cases) and impingement.
- **Key modification**: Prevent the shoulder from moving into extension by keeping the elbows just in front of the trunk at the lowest point of Chaturanga, bending the elbow to approximately 80°.
- **Eccentric loading**: Slowly lowering into Chaturanga with control builds tendon capacity, in contrast to passive stretching which can worsen tendinopathy.

### Scapular Dyskinesis
Abnormal scapular movement—often called "scapular winging"—disrupts the foundation of all upper extremity movement.
- **Observation**: The medial border of the scapula lifts off the rib cage during arm movement. This is visible from behind during Chaturanga, Handstand, or any weight-bearing arm elevation.
- **Role**: Poor scapular control reduces force transmission, increases impingement risk, and overloads the rotator cuff. It is often the root cause rather than a separate injury.
- **Key muscles**: The serratus anterior, lower trapezius, and rhomboids must co-contract to stabilize the scapula against the ribcage. Weakness in the serratus anterior is the most common contributor to winging.
- **Rehabilitation**: Serratus anterior activation via push-up plus (full protraction at the top of the push-up), wall slides, and scapular retraction exercises.

### Lateral Epicondylopathy (Tennis Elbow)
- **Mechanism**: Repetitive wrist extension in weight-bearing poses (Downward Dog, Plank, arm balances) overloads the common extensor tendon at the lateral epicondyle.
- **Symptoms**: Pain on the outside of the elbow, worse with gripping or extending the wrist against resistance. Tenderness directly over the lateral epicondyle.
- **Yoga modifications**: Make fists instead of palms in Downward Dog and Plank—this shortens the wrist extensors and reduces tendon strain. Use blocks under the hands. Reduce time in arm balances.
- **Eccentric loading**: Slow, controlled lowering of the wrist from extension to flexion (Tyler twist with a flexbar) is the gold standard for rehabilitation.

### Wrist Pain and Carpal Tunnel Syndrome
- **Mechanism**: Sustained wrist extension in weight-bearing poses compresses the carpal tunnel and loads the wrist ligaments. This is common in practitioners who lack wrist extension range of motion or who bear weight through the palm's base rather than distributing load across the whole hand.
- **Yoga modifications**: Use fists instead of palms in Downward Dog, Plank, and Chaturanga. Use blocks or wedges under the hands to reduce wrist extension angle. Perform Downward Dog on forearms (Dolphin) as an alternative.
- **Distinguish**: Carpal tunnel presents with numbness/tingling in the thumb, index, and middle fingers (median nerve distribution), often worse at night. Wrist ligament strain presents as focal pain over the dorsal wrist without neurologic symptoms.
